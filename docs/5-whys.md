# Five whys — a hypothesis chain

## Starting problem

A new user may not feel enough continuity or control to return after the first session. We have not measured this problem in the real product.

| Why | Working explanation | What would test it |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Why might the user not return? | The conversation may feel interchangeable rather than personally relevant. | Exit interviews and day-seven return by first-session experience. |
| 2. Why might it feel interchangeable? | A useful detail may not visibly shape the next reply. | Compare generic continuation with accurate recall. |
| 3. Why might recall fail to create relevance? | Remembering a fact is different from using it at a helpful moment. | Test recall timing while holding the detail constant. |
| 4. Why might visible recall still feel wrong? | The fact may be incorrect, unwanted or out of date. | Observe correction and forget actions, and ask about comfort. |
| 5. Why might those errors reduce trust? | The user may not know what is stored or how to change it. | Test a clear memory surface and discoverable controls. |

## Proposed root

A missing feeling of continuity and control, not merely a missing ability to repeat facts. This is our inference, not a verified root cause.

## Evidence boundary

The [third-party review](https://shouldieventry.com/products/candy-ai-review/) is a directional signal. The [public job post](https://jobs.ashbyhq.com/everai/67415245-9710-4d32-a544-735d779e1d44) supplies business context. Neither establishes this causal chain.

## Competing explanations

Companion fit, pricing, response speed, repetitive conversation, unclear expectations and onboarding friction could explain return behaviour. A memory experiment should compete with these explanations, not assume them away.

## Falsification

If accurate, user-controlled recall improves neither first-session confidence nor day-seven return, deprioritise this intervention. If people find it intrusive, revise or stop even when engagement rises.
